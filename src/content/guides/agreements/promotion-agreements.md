## The right that catches promotion deals

Practice guide 84’s fourth category is a legal right to direct or request a sale or lease, which HMLR notes is often found in land promotion agreements. It reflects how promotion works: the promoter secures planning, then requires or requests the landowner to sell to a buyer the promoter has found, and takes a share of the proceeds.

Whether a given agreement is caught depends on its substance. The MHCLG guidance is clear that an agreement does not need to use the word “development”, and HMLR says the exemptions should be read narrowly.

## One agreement, several rights

Promotion agreements rarely contain just one right. A typical agreement might include:

- the right to **direct a sale** once planning is granted;
- a **fallback option** for the promoter to buy the land itself;
- a **pre-emption** if the landowner wants to sell during the term;
- a **lease option** for a site compound.

HMLR’s scenarios guidance says the Regulations apply to each right individually, not to the contract as a whole. Each right that meets the criteria needs its own information, and each has its own clocks for assignment, variation and ending. One conveyancer can provide the information for several rights under one contract.

## The term, and the 18-month exemption

Promotion agreements commonly run for years, with extensions tied to planning appeals. Count every extension when you test the short-term exemption: if the right could last 18 months or more, it is not short-term. When you report, give the initial period of control (contract date to the latest exercise date, ignoring extensions), then the extension and termination provisions.

## When it ends

For a right to direct or request a sale, Practice guide 84 treats the right as exercised when the disposition is completed. The promoter reports the ending within 60 days. A promotion agreement that is terminated or simply expires is also reported within 60 days.

## What it means for promoters from 2028

Once the dataset is public, a promoter’s whole portfolio becomes legible: who you are working with, where, and for how long. Landowners being approached by rival promoters will be able to see who already controls the neighbouring fields. Plan for that in how you approach new sites.
