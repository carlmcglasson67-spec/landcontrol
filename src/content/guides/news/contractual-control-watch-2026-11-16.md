## The short version

Nothing has changed at source. Practice guide 84, the scenarios and the [Regulations](/news/regulations-made/) all stand as they were on 26 October, and the technical guidance for the HMLR Portal and Business Gateway has not yet appeared. What has moved is practice. Three briefings published since 21 October, from [CMS](https://cms.law/en/media/international/files/legal-briefing-on-contractual-control-agreements-november-2026), [Freeths](https://www.freeths.co.uk/insights-events/legal-articles/2026/hm-land-registry-contractual-control-rights-reporting-further-guidance/) and HB Public Law in [Local Government Lawyer](https://localgovernmentlawyer.co.uk/property/313-property-features/102182-land-agreements-and-the-new-contractual-control-regime), point the same way: the work that decides whether a land team meets its deadlines is happening now, in contracts and data collection, not in April 2027.

## 1. Contracts are starting to allocate the job

CMS reports that new agreements increasingly oblige the grantor to assist the grantee with its reporting obligations. HB Public Law makes a related recommendation: the contract should state who is responsible for registration.

**Our take.** This is the most useful signal of the month. The duty sits with the grantee, but much of the [information HMLR needs](/hmlr/information-required/) is held by the grantor. A short cooperation clause costs little at heads of terms and avoids a scramble later, particularly on variations and assignments, where a fresh 60-day clock runs. Landowners and their agents can expect to see these clauses, and it is worth reading them for scope: cooperating with information requests is one thing; taking on the grantee's statutory duty is another.

## 2. Birth details are the awkward data point

Freeths points out that for individuals, date and place of birth will be needed, and recommends a process for gathering it early in the deal. CMS puts it in terms of the grantor, where the grantor is an individual, and notes possible relief where getting the details is unreasonable, giving the example of an assignment made without the landowner's involvement. These details are used for verification and not published, as both CMS and Practice guide 84 make clear.

**Our take.** For rights granted from 8 June 2026, the easiest moment to collect birth details is before exchange, while the landowner is engaged. Promoters holding options over farmland owned by families, executors or several individuals may want to add it to their onboarding checklist now. Those rights must be reported [before 6 October 2027](/compliance/transitional-period/), and chasing a landowner a year after signing is harder than asking at the outset.

## 3. Plans need to be data, not drawings

Freeths expects HMLR to require digital mapping data rather than a scanned plan where a right covers only part of a title, with GeoJSON, Shapefile or online mapping tools as the routes in. CMS adds that coordinates must be in British National Grid. Freeths also asks, without answering, whether this could signal a wider shift in how HMLR expects land to be registered.

**Our take.** That wider question is speculation for now; nothing HMLR has published says so. The immediate point is narrower: on part-title deals, it makes sense to ask the surveyor or plan drafter for a [GeoJSON or Shapefile](/spatial-data/geojson-shapefile-british-national-grid/) at the same time as the plan, not afterwards.

## 4. A first look from the council side

The HB Public Law article, by V. Charles Ward, is the first substantial piece we have seen written for local authority conveyancers. It recommends checking nomination agreements where a council keeps nomination rights over a future development, and notes that applications can only be made by a UK registered conveyancing practitioner.

**Our take.** The council angle is underserved. On our reading of the Regulations, councils can sit on either side of a caught right, for example when conditionally buying land for regeneration or selling surplus land subject to conditions. In-house legal teams will also want to check they have an [individually regulated conveyancer](/hmlr/who-can-submit/) able to submit. Two cautions when reading the piece:

- **Start date.** It gives the start of the retrospective window as both 8 June and 8 April 2026. The correct date is 8 June 2026, the day [SI 2026/615](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2026/615) was made, as Practice guide 84 confirms.
- **Section 106.** It treats section 106 obligations involving a future transfer, including highways or open space, as reportable. Practice guide 84 section 3.2.5 exempts rights relating exclusively to infrastructure, amenities or services connected with a planning permission, while HMLR's scenario 8 says an affordable housing right falls outside that exemption. A transfer of land for a road or open space is worth testing against the [exemption](/agreements/exemptions/) before assuming it must be reported.

## 5. Where CMS lands on the hard questions

CMS takes a position on three points practitioners keep raising:

- **An open question:** whether a conditional contract is caught where completion depends on a landlord reaching practical completion of works. CMS treats this as uncertain.
- **A view:** an option held purely for growth in land value is unlikely, in CMS's view, to benefit from the non-development exemption.
- **A reading:** an assignment is a trigger event even if the right has stopped being a contractual control right by then.

**Our take.** The second point is consistent with HMLR's scenario 6 on investment options, which we cover in the [scenarios guide](/hmlr/contractual-control-scenarios/). The first remains open. Practice guide 84 does not say what counts as a reasonable excuse for not reporting, so where a right is borderline, a dated note of the reasoning and the guidance relied on is a sensible record to keep.

## A note on what is not changing

Price and financial terms are not part of the information required, and the underlying agreement is not submitted, as CMS notes. Practice guide 84's list of required information does not include price, and it asks grantees to keep a copy of the contract so they can answer HMLR's questions, rather than send it in.
